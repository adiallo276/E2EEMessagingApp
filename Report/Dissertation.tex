\begin{abstract}
\noindent
The advent of scalable quantum computing poses an existential threat to the public-key
cryptographic infrastructure underpinning modern digital communications. Shor's
algorithm~\cite{shor1994} reduces the factorisation of RSA moduli and the computation
of elliptic curve discrete logarithms to polynomial-time quantum circuits, rendering
RSA-2048 and ECDH-256---the workhorses of contemporary secure messaging---trivially
breakable by a sufficiently powerful quantum adversary. This threat is not merely
hypothetical: ``harvest now, decrypt later'' (HNDL) attacks motivate immediate
migration even before cryptographically relevant quantum computers exist.

This dissertation presents the design, implementation, and systematic empirical
evaluation of three post-quantum Key Encapsulation Mechanisms (KEMs)---Mini-Kyber
(Module-LWE), Mini-Frodo (plain LWE), and Mini-NTRU (NTRU lattice)---deployed within
a fully functional real-time messaging application providing genuine end-to-end
encryption (E2EE). The system is implemented using Spring Boot 3.2 on the backend and
Next.js~14/TypeScript on the frontend, with all cryptographic operations executing
client-side in the browser, enforcing the E2EE security boundary.

Comprehensive benchmarking comprising 5,000 KEM invocations per algorithm and a
500-session throughput trial, analysed via Welch's $t$-tests with 95\% confidence
intervals, demonstrates that post-quantum algorithms impose no measurable performance
penalty at session level. Mini-Kyber is statistically significantly faster than ECDH
at session level ($t = -7.44$, $p \ll 0.001$), with the 9\,ms absolute difference
being practically imperceptible. An automated test suite of 21 tests identified and
confirmed the resolution of four implementation bugs, including a ${\sim}55\%$
decapsulation failure rate caused by erroneous averaging of polynomial coefficients
under modular arithmetic, demonstrating that cryptographic unit testing is not optional
but essential. The dissertation positions this work as a fourth benchmark environment
for PQC algorithms---pure TypeScript JIT---alongside existing native C, embedded ARM,
and WASM reference environments.
\end{abstract}

\section{Motivation and Context}

The security of the global communications infrastructure rests on two computational
hardness assumptions: the difficulty of integer factorisation (RSA) and the discrete
logarithm problem over elliptic curves (ECDH, ECDSA). These assumptions have sustained
decades of cryptanalytic scrutiny and underpin TLS~1.3, Signal, WhatsApp, iMessage,
and virtually every other secure messaging protocol deployed at scale. Their security
is well-understood: the best classical algorithms for both problems require
sub-exponential time, making them computationally infeasible for key sizes in common
use.

In 1994, Peter Shor demonstrated that a quantum computer could solve both problems in
polynomial time~\cite{shor1994}. The quantum Fourier transform enables solving the
hidden subgroup problem over abelian groups in $O((\log N)^3)$ operations---an
exponential improvement over the best classical algorithms. The implication is
unambiguous: a sufficiently powerful quantum computer would render all RSA and
elliptic-curve cryptography trivially breakable.

The practical timeline for cryptographically relevant quantum computers (CRQCs) is
contested among researchers, with credible estimates ranging from 2030 to
2040~\cite{nist2024}. However, this temporal uncertainty does not diminish the urgency
of migration. The ``harvest now, decrypt later'' attack model has already been
operationalised: sophisticated adversaries intercept and archive encrypted network
traffic today, with the expectation of decrypting it once CRQCs become available.
Medical records, financial transactions, diplomatic communications, and personal
correspondence encrypted under RSA or ECDH today are potentially exposed, regardless
of when quantum computers arrive. Data with confidentiality requirements exceeding ten
years---a category that includes much government, healthcare, and financial
data---should already be considered at risk.

\section{Research Objectives}

This dissertation addresses two primary research questions:

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{RQ\arabic*:}]
  \item \textbf{Performance:} Do post-quantum Key Encapsulation Mechanisms impose a
  measurable performance overhead compared to ECDH in a real-time browser-based E2EE
  messaging application, when measured across KEM operations, session establishment,
  and sustained throughput?

  \item \textbf{Comparative Analysis:} Do the empirically observed performance
  characteristics of Mini-Kyber (Module-LWE), Mini-Frodo (plain LWE), and Mini-NTRU
  (NTRU lattice) in a TypeScript browser environment preserve the relative rankings
  established in production benchmarking literature, validating reduced-parameter
  implementations as a methodology for comparative algorithm evaluation?
\end{enumerate}

Secondary objectives include: constructing a working end-to-end encrypted messaging
application demonstrating practical PQC deployability; producing a statistically
rigorous benchmark methodology with honest treatment of JIT variability and effective
sample sizes; and identifying and resolving implementation bugs through automated
correctness testing.

\section{Contributions}

This dissertation makes the following original contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A fourth PQC benchmark environment.} Existing literature benchmarks
  PQC algorithms in native C~\cite{crystals_kyber_spec}, on embedded ARM
  hardware~\cite{pqm4}, and via WASM compilation. This work contributes measurements
  in a pure TypeScript JIT environment---the runtime context relevant to browser-based
  messaging applications---under a consistent measurement infrastructure enabling
  direct algorithmic comparison.

  \item \textbf{A working E2EE messaging application with interchangeable PQC
  algorithms.} The system implements a unified KEM interface behind which Kyber, Frodo,
  NTRU, and ECDH are interchangeable, demonstrating that PQC integration into
  production-grade messaging architecture is architecturally feasible.

  \item \textbf{Statistical rigour in browser PQC benchmarking.} The evaluation
  employs Welch's $t$-tests with correct effective sample sizes (n$_{\text{eff}}=500$),
  distinguishing statistical significance from practical significance, and reporting
  non-significant results honestly.

  \item \textbf{Automated correctness testing revealing four critical implementation
  bugs.} The 100-iteration correctness sweep identified bugs with ${\sim}55\%$
  decapsulation failure rates that were entirely invisible in manual integration
  testing, demonstrating the necessity of unit-level cryptographic correctness
  validation.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Lattice Cryptography: Foundations}

\subsection{Lattices and Hard Problems}

\begin{definition}[Lattice]
A lattice $\Lambda$ is a discrete additive subgroup of $\mathbb{R}^n$. Given linearly
independent basis vectors $\mathbf{b}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{b}_k \in \mathbb{R}^n$, the
lattice generated by this basis is:
\[
\Lambda(\mathbf{b}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{b}_k) = \left\{ \sum_{i=1}^{k} z_i \mathbf{b}_i \;\middle|\; z_i \in \mathbb{Z} \right\}
\]
\end{definition}

The canonical computational problems on lattices that underpin modern PQC are:

\begin{definition}[Shortest Vector Problem (SVP)]
Given a lattice basis $\mathbf{B}$, find the shortest non-zero vector $\mathbf{v} \in \Lambda(\mathbf{B})$,
i.e., find $\mathbf{v}$ minimising $\|\mathbf{v}\|$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Closest Vector Problem (CVP)]
Given a lattice basis $\mathbf{B}$ and a target vector $\mathbf{t} \in \mathbb{R}^n$,
find the lattice vector $\mathbf{v} \in \Lambda(\mathbf{B})$ minimising $\|\mathbf{v} - \mathbf{t}\|$.
\end{definition}

Both SVP and CVP are NP-hard in the worst case~\cite{peikert2016}. Critically for
post-quantum security, no polynomial-time quantum algorithm is known for either
problem. The best quantum algorithms for SVP---variants of BKZ enhanced with quantum
random walks---achieve complexity $2^{0.265n}$, compared to the best classical $2^{0.292n}$.
This represents a constant-factor improvement, not the exponential speedup that
Shor's algorithm provides for factorisation. Doubling lattice dimensions provides
sufficient quantum resistance.

\subsection{Learning With Errors}

The Learning With Errors problem, introduced by Regev~\cite{regev2005}, is the
algebraic instantiation of lattice hardness that underpins Kyber and Frodo.

\begin{definition}[Learning With Errors (LWE)]
Let $n, q \geq 1$ be integers and $\chi$ be a probability distribution over $\mathbb{Z}$.
The LWE distribution $A_{\mathbf{s},\chi}$ for a secret $\mathbf{s} \in \mathbb{Z}_q^n$
samples $\mathbf{a} \leftarrow \mathbb{Z}_q^n$ uniformly and $e \leftarrow \chi$,
returning $(\mathbf{a}, \langle \mathbf{a}, \mathbf{s} \rangle + e \pmod{q})$.

The (decisional) LWE problem is to distinguish $m$ samples from $A_{\mathbf{s},\chi}$
from $m$ uniformly random samples in $\mathbb{Z}_q^n \times \mathbb{Z}_q$.
\end{definition}

In matrix form: given $\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{Z}_q^{m \times n}$,
$\mathbf{s} \in \mathbb{Z}_q^n$, and $\mathbf{e} \in \mathbb{Z}_q^m$ with small entries,
distinguish $\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{s} + \mathbf{e} \pmod{q}$ from uniformly
random $\mathbf{b}$. The error vector $\mathbf{e}$ is the critical ingredient:
without it, $\mathbf{s}$ is recoverable by Gaussian elimination in polynomial time.

The fundamental security guarantee of LWE comes from Regev's quantum reduction:

\begin{theorem}[Regev's Reduction~\cite{regev2005}]
If there exists an efficient algorithm solving the decisional LWE problem with
parameters $(n, q, \chi)$ where $q$ is a prime bounded by a polynomial in $n$ and
$\chi$ is a discrete Gaussian with standard deviation $\sigma \geq 2\sqrt{n}$, then
there exists an efficient quantum algorithm solving GapSVP and SIVP on arbitrary
$n$-dimensional lattices with approximation factor $\tilde{O}(n/\sigma)$.
\end{theorem}

This reduction is quantum in the sense that the reduction algorithm uses a quantum
subroutine, meaning it shows that breaking LWE is at least as hard as solving
worst-case lattice problems with a quantum computer. The consequence is profound:
an attacker breaking LWE-based cryptography would simultaneously solve the hardest
instances of SVP for all lattices of the relevant dimension---a capability that would
also break every other lattice-based cryptosystem. No such algorithm is known.

\subsection{Ring-LWE and Module-LWE}

Standard LWE has key sizes of $O(n^2)$ due to the $n \times n$ matrix $\mathbf{A}$.
At $n = 640$ (FrodoKEM-640), public keys require approximately 640\,KB. This motivates
algebraically structured variants.

\begin{definition}[Ring-LWE~\cite{lyubashevsky2010}]
Let $R_q = \mathbb{Z}_q[x]/(f(x))$ for an irreducible polynomial $f(x)$ of degree $n$.
The Ring-LWE distribution samples $a \leftarrow R_q$ uniformly and $e \leftarrow \chi_R$
(a distribution over $R_q$ with small coefficients), returning $(a, as + e)$ for secret
$s \in R_q$.
\end{definition}

Kyber uses $f(x) = x^n + 1$, the $2n$-th cyclotomic polynomial, giving the ring
$R_q = \mathbb{Z}_q[x]/(x^n+1)$ where $n=256$ and $q=3329$. Multiplication in this
ring satisfies $x^n \equiv -1$, enabling the \emph{negacyclic} Number Theoretic
Transform (NTT) to compute polynomial multiplication in $O(n \log n)$ instead of
$O(n^2)$. Key sizes reduce from $O(n^2)$ to $O(n)$.

Module-LWE generalises Ring-LWE to modules over $R_q$, replacing scalar ring elements
with length-$k$ vectors of ring elements. Kyber uses $k=2$ (Kyber-512), $k=3$
(Kyber-768), or $k=4$ (Kyber-1024), providing a tunable security-efficiency tradeoff.
The public key is $(A, t = As + e)$ where $A \in R_q^{k \times k}$ and $s, e \in R_q^k$.

\begin{remark}
Frodo deliberately avoids ring structure, using a full $n \times n$ matrix $A$ over
$\mathbb{Z}_q$ with no algebraic assumptions. While this increases key sizes and
computational cost by a factor of $\Theta(n)$ compared to Kyber, it eliminates the
risk that ring structure might be exploitable by a future algebraic attack. No such
attack is known, but Frodo's conservatism provides a hedge against this unknown risk.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The NTRU Problem}

NTRU~\cite{hoffstein1998}, proposed in 1996 and the oldest surviving lattice-based
public-key cryptosystem, rests on an entirely different hardness assumption.

\begin{definition}[NTRU Problem]
Work in the ring $\mathcal{R} = \mathbb{Z}[x]/(x^N - 1)$ with modulus $q$. Let
$f, g \in \mathcal{R}$ be short polynomials with coefficients in $\{-1, 0, 1\}$.
The NTRU problem is: given the public polynomial $h = f^{-1} \cdot g \pmod{q}$,
find short polynomials $f'$ and $g'$ such that $h = f'^{-1} \cdot g' \pmod{q}$.
\end{definition}

The security relies on the difficulty of recovering the short polynomials from their
``ratio'' $h$. This is related to the SVP on a specific class of NTRU lattices but is
formally distinct from LWE: no reduction between NTRU and LWE is known in either
direction. NTRU thus provides an \emph{independent} hardness assumption.

Note that NTRU uses the ring $\mathbb{Z}[x]/(x^N - 1)$, where $x^N \equiv +1$
(cyclic convolution), whereas Kyber uses $\mathbb{Z}_q[x]/(x^n + 1)$ where
$x^n \equiv -1$ (negacyclic convolution). This algebraic difference has implications
for both security analysis and implementation: cyclic polynomial multiplication does
not support the standard NTT, and the ring structure admits different lattice reduction
strategies.

\section{Quantum Resistance of Lattice Problems}

The key distinction between lattice-based cryptography and classical public-key
cryptography is the absence of a polynomial-time quantum algorithm for lattice problems.

\textbf{Why Shor's algorithm does not apply:} Shor's algorithm solves the hidden
subgroup problem (HSP) over abelian groups. Integer factorisation reduces to HSP over
$\mathbb{Z}$; the elliptic curve discrete logarithm reduces to HSP over elliptic curve
groups. Both groups are abelian, and the quantum Fourier transform over abelian groups
is efficiently computable, enabling polynomial-time quantum algorithms.

SVP and CVP do not reduce to HSP over any known abelian group. The lattice shortest
vector problem has a non-abelian symmetry structure that resists the quantum Fourier
transform approach. Despite significant research effort---including attempts to
formulate lattice problems as non-abelian HSP instances---no polynomial-time quantum
reduction has been found~\cite{peikert2016}.

\textbf{Grover's algorithm provides only quadratic speedup:} Grover's unstructured
search provides a quadratic quantum speedup, finding a marked element among $N$ items
in $O(\sqrt{N})$ quantum operations~\cite{bernstein2017}. For symmetric cryptography
(AES) and hash functions, this is mitigated by doubling key sizes (AES-256 remains
secure). For lattice problems, the best quantum algorithms based on Grover search
achieve complexity approximately $2^{0.265n}$ (BKZ with quantum speedup), compared to
$2^{0.292n}$ classically---an improvement of roughly $12\%$ in exponent, not the
exponential collapse that Shor provides for RSA. Increasing lattice dimensions by
a modest factor (e.g., from $n=512$ to $n=768$) suffices to compensate.

\section{Key Encapsulation Mechanisms}

A Key Encapsulation Mechanism is a tuple of algorithms (KeyGen, Encaps, Decaps)
providing a method for two parties to establish a shared secret, where one party
generates a keypair and the other ``encapsulates'' a random secret that only the
keypair holder can recover.

\begin{definition}[KEM Security: IND-CCA2]
A KEM is \emph{indistinguishable under adaptive chosen-ciphertext attack} (IND-CCA2)
if no probabilistic polynomial-time adversary can distinguish real from random shared
secrets, even when given access to a decapsulation oracle for all ciphertexts except
the challenge. Formally, the adversary's advantage:
\[
\text{Adv}^{\text{IND-CCA2}}_\text{KEM}(\mathcal{A}) = \left| \Pr[\mathcal{A}^{\text{Decaps}(\cdot)}(pk, ct^*, K^*) = b] - \frac{1}{2} \right| \leq \text{negl}(n)
\]
\end{definition}

IND-CCA2 security is the gold standard for KEMs in real-world deployment. It is
achieved from IND-CPA secure schemes via the Fujisaki-Okamoto (FO)
transform~\cite{nist2024}, which adds a re-encryption check during decapsulation to
resist active adversaries. The Mini implementations in this project achieve correctness
and computational security relative to their parameters; full IND-CCA2 with FO
transform is provided by the production wrapper (\texttt{production-kem.ts}) using
\texttt{@noble/post-quantum}~\cite{noble_pq}.

\section{NIST Post-Quantum Standardisation}

The National Institute of Standards and Technology initiated a public competition for
post-quantum cryptographic standards in 2016, culminating in the publication of three
primary standards in August 2024~\cite{nist2024}:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{FIPS 203}: ML-KEM, based on CRYSTALS-Kyber~\cite{crystals_kyber_spec}
  \item \textbf{FIPS 204}: ML-DSA, based on CRYSTALS-Dilithium
  \item \textbf{FIPS 205}: SLH-DSA, based on SPHINCS+
\end{itemize}

This dissertation focuses exclusively on KEMs (FIPS~203), as key encapsulation is the
component directly relevant to E2EE session establishment. Signature schemes (FIPS~204,
205) are excluded as out of scope.

\textbf{Algorithm selection rationale:} The three algorithms in this dissertation were
chosen to represent three independent hardness assumptions and three distinct
performance profiles. BIKE, HQC, and Classic McEliece (code-based schemes) were
excluded due to key sizes in the range of kilobytes to megabytes, making them
impractical for browser-based deployment. SPHINCS+ and Dilithium are signature schemes,
not KEMs.

\section{PQC in Production Systems}

\textbf{Signal Protocol PQXDH:} In September 2023, Signal deployed the
Post-Quantum Extended Diffie-Hellman (PQXDH) protocol~\cite{signal_pqxdh}, replacing
their classical X3DH key agreement with a hybrid construction combining X25519 and
CRYSTALS-Kyber-1024. The hybrid design provides security if either primitive is
secure: post-quantum security against future CRQCs, and classical security against
any weaknesses in early Kyber deployments. Signal's deployment represents the most
significant production deployment of PQC in consumer messaging to date.

\textbf{Google Chrome ML-KEM:} In November 2024, Google Chrome began deploying hybrid
ML-KEM key exchange in TLS~1.3~\cite{tls13}, using X25519MLKEM768---a hybrid of
X25519 and ML-KEM-768. Cloudflare subsequently reported that approximately 38\% of
human HTTPS traffic traversing its network used hybrid post-quantum handshakes by
early 2025~\cite{nist_migration}. This adoption trajectory demonstrates that lattice
cryptography is already deployed at internet scale.

\textbf{Architectural comparison:} This project diverges from Signal's PQXDH in two
important respects. First, it isolates the PQC component to enable clean algorithmic
comparison: Signal's hybrid design deliberately conflates PQC and classical security,
making pure PQC performance characterisation impossible from deployment data. Second,
the browser JavaScript execution environment introduces constraints absent from Signal's
native application context: JIT non-determinism, garbage collection pauses, and the
absence of SIMD instruction access.

\section{Browser-Based PQC Implementations}

The primary approach to browser PQC in the literature and industry is WebAssembly
(WASM) compilation of native C libraries. The Open Quantum Safe project provides
liboqs, a C implementation of all NIST candidates compilable to WASM via Emscripten.
The pqm4 project~\cite{pqm4} benchmarks the same algorithms on ARM Cortex-M4
embedded targets.

Both approaches introduce a confounding variable: the FFI boundary between JavaScript
and WASM involves memory copying between the JavaScript heap and WASM linear memory,
adding overhead that is difficult to separate from algorithm cost. For pure comparative
benchmarking---determining which algorithm is faster, and by how much---this overhead
contaminates measurements. The \texttt{@noble/post-quantum} library~\cite{noble_pq}
provides the only production-grade, security-audited, pure-JavaScript PQC
implementation, operating entirely within the JavaScript engine and therefore directly
comparable to Mini implementations in terms of runtime environment.

\textbf{Positioning:} This project contributes a fourth benchmark environment:
pure TypeScript in a JIT-compiled browser engine. The comparative methodology---three
algorithm families under identical measurement infrastructure with Welch's $t$-test
significance analysis---represents, to the author's knowledge, the first such
comparison published for this execution environment.

\section{Technology Stack}

\subsection{Backend}

The server is implemented in Java~21 with Spring Boot~3.4. The key framework
components and their justifications are:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Spring Security with JWT:} Provides a production-grade authentication
  filter chain. JWT tokens are issued on login and validated at both the HTTP handshake
  and on every STOMP frame via two interceptor layers (\texttt{JwtHandshakeInterceptor}
  and \texttt{StompAuthChannelInterceptor}), preventing connection hijacking.

  \item \textbf{Spring WebSocket with STOMP:} Raw WebSockets provide only a byte
  channel. STOMP adds subscription semantics, topic routing, and message framing.
  Clients subscribe to \texttt{/topic/conversations/\{id\}} and the server routes
  messages via \texttt{SimpMessagingTemplate}. SockJS provides transparent fallback to
  HTTP long-polling when WebSocket connections are blocked by firewalls.

  \item \textbf{Spring Data JPA with H2:} H2 in-memory database requires zero
  configuration, with Hibernate auto-generating the schema from JPA entities.
  Switching to PostgreSQL requires changing a single property in
  \texttt{application.properties}.

  \item \textbf{Java 21 Virtual Threads:} Project Loom virtual threads enable handling
  many concurrent WebSocket connections without blocking OS threads, improving
  scalability for a real-time messaging workload.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Frontend}

The client is implemented in Next.js~14 with TypeScript and React~18. Relevant
architectural decisions:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Next.js App Router API routes:} Server-side only execution ensures the
  OpenAI API key never touches the browser. The \texttt{/app/api/chat/route.ts} handler
  reads the key via \texttt{process.env.OPENAI\_API\_KEY} and is never bundled into
  client JavaScript.

  \item \textbf{TypeScript:} Type safety across the KEM interface (\texttt{keyGen},
  \texttt{encapsulate}, \texttt{decapsulate}) enforced at compile time. Interface
  mismatches between algorithm implementations are caught before reaching the browser.

  \item \textbf{Web Crypto API:} The browser's \texttt{crypto.getRandomValues()} is
  used exclusively for all randomness, replacing an earlier \texttt{Math.random()} usage
  in Mini-NTRU identified and corrected during security review.
\end{itemize}

\section{E2EE Protocol Design}

\subsection{Security Model}

The protocol provides end-to-end encryption with the following properties:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Forward secrecy (session-level):} Each conversation establishes a
  unique session key derived via HKDF with a conversation-specific context string.

  \item \textbf{Server blindness:} The server stores only AES-256-GCM ciphertexts
  and public keys. It never holds a shared secret, session key, or plaintext.

  \item \textbf{PQC-hardened key agreement:} The session key derives from a
  post-quantum KEM shared secret, providing quantum resistance from session establishment
  through all subsequent message encryption.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Acknowledged limitations:} Secret keys are stored in \texttt{localStorage},
which is readable by any JavaScript executing in the page context. An XSS vulnerability
could therefore exfiltrate secret keys. Production deployment would use the Web Crypto
API's non-extractable \texttt{CryptoKey} objects, which are stored in the browser's
secure key store and cannot be read by JavaScript. This limitation is explicitly
documented and is a prototype implementation choice, not a fundamental design flaw.

\subsection{Session Key Derivation}

The shared KEM secret $K$ is not used directly as an AES key. HKDF~\cite{webcrypto}
is applied with three distinct inputs:

\begin{align}
K_{\text{session}} &= \texttt{HKDF}(K,\; \mathit{salt},\; \mathit{info}) \\
\mathit{info} &= \texttt{"messaging-e2ee-v1:}\mathit{alg}\texttt{:conv:}\mathit{conversationId}\texttt{"}
\end{align}

Three properties are achieved:

\textbf{Domain separation:} Including $\mathit{alg}$ and $\mathit{conversationId}$ in
the HKDF info string ensures that even if two conversations produce the same raw KEM
output $K$ (unlikely but theoretically possible), they derive completely different
session keys. Without domain separation, two contexts sharing the same $K$ would share
the same $K_{\text{session}}$---a potentially catastrophic vulnerability.

\textbf{Key stretching:} The raw KEM output may not have the distribution required by
AES-GCM. HKDF extracts and expands it into a properly formatted 256-bit key.

\textbf{Forward compatibility:} The version string \texttt{messaging-e2ee-v1} enables
future protocol versions to derive incompatible keys from the same KEM infrastructure.

\subsection{Message Encryption}

Every plaintext message $m$ is encrypted as:
\[
c = \texttt{AES-256-GCM}_{K_{\text{session}}}(m,\; IV)
\]
where $IV$ is a fresh 12-byte random nonce generated by \texttt{crypto.getRandomValues()}
for each message. AES-GCM provides both confidentiality and authenticity: the
authentication tag ensures that a message encrypted under a mismatched session key
fails decryption with overwhelming probability, a property exploited by the automated
correctness testing (Section~\ref{sec:testing}).

\section{Design Philosophy: Mini Implementations}

\subsection{Rationale}

Full production implementations of Kyber, Frodo, and NTRU were not viable within the
browser JavaScript environment for three specific technical reasons:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{NTT complexity:} Full Kyber requires a Number Theoretic Transform
  using bit-reversal permutation and Montgomery modular reduction. Implementing this
  correctly in JavaScript within the project timeline was not feasible. More
  importantly, using a WASM-compiled liboqs would introduce FFI boundary overhead
  confounding benchmarks.

  \item \textbf{JIT non-determinism and constant-time impossibility:} JavaScript's
  JIT compiler cannot guarantee constant-time execution. Timing side channels are
  therefore inherent to any JavaScript PQC implementation. This applies equally to
  Mini and full implementations, making Mini implementations equally valid for the
  purpose of measuring algorithmic performance characteristics.

  \item \textbf{Comparative benchmarking objective:} The research objective is
  \emph{relative} comparison of algorithm families, not absolute timing benchmarks.
  For this purpose, transparent TypeScript implementations sharing an identical
  execution environment are arguably preferable to opaque WASM-compiled libraries,
  as the only variable between algorithm measurements is the algorithm itself.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Mini-Kyber: Module-LWE}

\subsection{Key Generation}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Mini-Kyber Key Generation}
\label{alg:kyber-keygen}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State Sample $a \leftarrow R_q$ uniformly (public matrix element)
\State Sample $s \leftarrow \chi^k$ (secret vector, small coefficients)
\State Sample $e \leftarrow \chi^k$ (error vector, small coefficients)
\State Compute $t = a \cdot s + e \pmod{q}$ (public key element)
\State \Return $pk = (a, t)$, $sk = s$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The public key $(a, t)$ is a Ring-LWE instance: recovering $s$ from $(a, t)$ requires
solving Ring-LWE, which reduces to hard lattice problems~\cite{lyubashevsky2010}.

\subsection{Encapsulation and Message Encoding}

The critical implementation detail for correctness is the message encoding scheme.
A byte $m \in \{0, \ldots, 255\}$ is encoded across $N = 8$ polynomial coefficients:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Java, caption={Mini-Kyber message encoding},
                   label={lst:encode}]
function encodeMessage(m: number): Polynomial {
  const c = new Array(N).fill(0);
  const halfQ = Math.floor(Q / 2); // = 1664 for Q=3329
  for (let i = 0; i < N; i++) {
    c[i] = ((m >> i) & 1) === 1 ? halfQ : 0;
  }
  return new Polynomial(c);
}
\end{lstlisting}

Bit $i$ of $m$ is encoded as coefficient $i$ of the polynomial: $Q/2 = 1664$ for
bit=1, and $0$ for bit=0. After the LWE operations in encapsulation and decapsulation,
the recovered polynomial has coefficients approximately equal to $0$ or $1664$,
perturbed by small noise.

\subsection{Decapsulation and the Threshold Decoder}

After decapsulation recovers $w \approx \mathit{mEnc} + \text{noise}$, each
coefficient is decoded via a threshold rule:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Java, caption={Mini-Kyber threshold decoder
(corrected)}, label={lst:decode}]
function decodeMessage(w: Polynomial): number {
  const c = w.getCoeffs();
  let m = 0;
  for (let i = 0; i < c.length; i++) {
    let v = c[i] % Q;
    if (v < 0) v += Q;              // normalise to [0, Q)
    if (v > Q / 4 && v < 3 * Q / 4) {   // threshold: [Q/4, 3Q/4)
      m |= (1 << i);                // bit i = 1
    }
  }
  return m;
}
\end{lstlisting}

The threshold $[Q/4, 3Q/4) = [832, 2497)$ captures all values close to $Q/2 = 1664$.
The geometric justification: $0$ and $Q/2$ are the two maximally separated points in
$\mathbb{Z}_Q$, at Hamming distance $Q/2$ from each other. Noise magnitude is bounded
by the LWE error distribution ($\sigma \ll Q/4$), ensuring values near $Q/2$ never
cross the $Q/4$ or $3Q/4$ boundaries.

\section{Mini-Frodo: Plain LWE}

\subsection{Matrix-Vector Operations}

Frodo's security relies on the hardness of plain LWE with no ring structure. The
implementation uses a matrix class with explicit dimension tracking:

\textbf{KeyGen:} Sample $A \in \mathbb{Z}_q^{n \times n}$ uniformly, sample secret
$S \in \mathbb{Z}_q^{n \times \bar{n}}$ and error $E$ with small entries, compute
$B = AS + E \pmod{q}$. Public key: $(A, B)$, secret key: $S$.

\textbf{Encapsulation:} Sample $r \in \mathbb{Z}_q^{n}$ small, compute
$U = A^T r + e_1$ and $V = B^T r + e_2 + \mathit{mEnc}$.

\textbf{Decapsulation:} Compute $W = V - S^T U$. By algebraic cancellation:
\[
W = V - S^T U = (B^T r + e_2 + \mathit{mEnc}) - S^T(A^T r + e_1)
\]
Since $B = AS + E$, so $B^T = S^T A^T + E^T$:
\[
W = S^T A^T r + E^T r + e_2 + \mathit{mEnc} - S^T A^T r - S^T e_1
  = \mathit{mEnc} + \underbrace{(E^T r + e_2 - S^T e_1)}_{\text{small noise}}
\]
The $S^T A^T r$ terms cancel exactly. The residual noise $(E^T r + e_2 - S^T e_1)$
is small because $E$, $r$, $e_1$, $e_2$, and $S$ all have small coefficients.
The same $[Q/4, 3Q/4)$ threshold decoder then recovers $\mathit{mEnc}$ correctly.

\section{Mini-NTRU: Cyclic Ring}

\subsection{Polynomial Arithmetic}

NTRU operates via cyclic convolution in $\mathbb{Z}[x]/(x^N - 1)$, where $x^N \equiv +1$.
The implementation computes:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Java, caption={Cyclic polynomial multiplication},
                   label={lst:polmul}]
function polyMul(a: number[], b: number[], N: number, q: number): number[] {
  const result = new Array(N).fill(0);
  for (let i = 0; i < N; i++) {
    for (let j = 0; j < N; j++) {
      const k = (i + j) % N;     // cyclic: wraps with POSITIVE sign
      result[k] = ((result[k] + a[i] * b[j]) % q + q) % q;
    }
  }
  return result;
}
\end{lstlisting}

The critical property: when $i + j \geq N$, the result wraps to index $(i+j) \bmod N$
with a \emph{positive} sign (since $x^N = +1$). This contrasts with Kyber's
negacyclic ring where $x^n = -1$ causes wrapping terms to contribute negatively.
The ring difference has security implications: NTRU lattices have different geometric
properties from ideal lattices, requiring different cryptanalytic techniques.

\subsection{Key Generation and the Inversion Cost}

NTRU key generation requires computing $f^{-1} \pmod{q}$ using the extended
Euclidean algorithm over polynomial rings---computationally the most expensive
operation in the Mini-NTRU implementation. At $N = 7$, this inversion dominates
the entire key generation time despite the ring's small size. At production $N = 509$,
the inversion cost amortises because encapsulation's polynomial multiplication
$h \cdot r$ grows as $O(N^2)$ and becomes the dominant cost. This parameter-scale
effect explains the seemingly counterintuitive result that Mini-NTRU encapsulation is
\emph{slower} than production NTRU despite having far smaller parameters
(Section~\ref{sec:ntru-discrepancy}).

\section{The Testing Approach}

The automated test suite (\texttt{frontend/\_\_tests\_\_/crypto.test.ts}) was written
after initial implementation and before any attempt at performance benchmarking.
This sequence was deliberate: benchmarking an incorrect implementation produces
meaningless data. The suite comprises 21 tests across five categories:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Structural tests:} Validate keypair field presence and array dimensions.
  \item \textbf{Single-shot correctness:} Verify that one encapsulation/decapsulation
  cycle produces matching shared secrets.
  \item \textbf{100-iteration correctness sweeps:} Run 100 independent KEM cycles per
  algorithm, asserting 0 failures. This directly exposed Bugs 2 and 4.
  \item \textbf{Non-triviality:} Assert shared secrets are non-zero and non-constant.
  \item \textbf{Wrong-key resistance:} Verify that decapsulation with a different
  secret key produces a different shared secret. This exposed Bug~3.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Bug 1: AES Buffer Aliasing}

\textbf{File:} \texttt{frontend/lib/crypto/aes.ts}

\textbf{Root cause:} The \texttt{toArrayBuffer} function returned \texttt{u8.buffer}
directly. In JavaScript, a \texttt{Uint8Array} created by \texttt{crypto.getRandomValues()}
may be a \emph{view} into a larger parent buffer, with a non-zero byte offset. The
\texttt{.buffer} property returns the entire parent \texttt{ArrayBuffer}, not just
the view's range.

\textbf{Consequence:} When AES-GCM read the IV from the beginning of the returned
buffer, it was reading bytes from before the intended 12-byte IV---random garbage
from a shared buffer allocation. The ciphertext was constructed with an incorrect IV,
making decryption with the correct IV fail non-deterministically.

\textbf{Fix:}
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Java, caption={Buffer aliasing fix in aes.ts}]
// BEFORE (buggy):
function toArrayBuffer(u8: Uint8Array): ArrayBuffer {
  return u8.buffer; // may return parent buffer with wrong offset
}

// AFTER (correct):
function toArrayBuffer(u8: Uint8Array): ArrayBuffer {
  const ab = new ArrayBuffer(u8.byteLength);
  new Uint8Array(ab).set(u8); // copy into fresh, zero-offset buffer
  return ab;
}
\end{lstlisting}

\section{Bug 2: Kyber Decode Failure ($\sim$55\% Rate)}

\textbf{File:} \texttt{frontend/lib/crypto/minikyber.ts}

\textbf{Root cause:} The original \texttt{decodeMessage} function averaged all $N$
polynomial coefficients and compared the average to $Q/4$:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Java, caption={Buggy decoder (original)}]
// BEFORE (buggy):
function decodeMessage(w: Polynomial): number {
  const coeffs = w.getCoeffs();
  const avg = coeffs.reduce((a, b) => a + b, 0) / coeffs.length;
  return avg > Q / 4 ? 1 : 0; // single bit, averaged
}
\end{lstlisting}

This was incorrect for two reasons:

\textbf{(a) It recovered only one bit:} Averaging 8 coefficients and performing one
comparison produces a single bit. Since $m$ is a full byte (8 bits), 7 bits of
information were discarded. The shared secret was derived from a 1-bit value, giving
only two possible shared secrets ($m = 0$ or $m = 1$).

\textbf{(b) Modular wrap-around:} LWE noise is drawn from a distribution including
negative values. A coefficient near $0$ (bit=0) with noise $e = -5$ should be decoded
as bit=0. However, in $\mathbb{Z}_Q$, $-5 \equiv Q - 5 = 3324$. The value $3324$ is
near $Q$, not near $0$. When averaged with other coefficients, this dramatically
inflates the average, causing systematic misclassification.

\textbf{Failure rate:} Given the noise distribution, modular wrap-around of negative

wrong bit vector and therefore the wrong shared secret.

\textbf{Invisibility in integration testing:} AES-GCM with mismatched keys fails
authentication silently. A message encrypted under Alice's session key and decrypted
with Bob's (different) session key produces an authentication failure---the message
simply does not appear on Bob's screen. This is behaviourally identical to a network
drop. Manual integration testing---opening the app, sending messages, observing
receipt---cannot distinguish a 55\% KEM failure rate from intermittent network issues.
Only the automated 100-iteration correctness sweep, which explicitly compares both
parties' shared secrets, reliably exposed the failure.

\textbf{Fix:} Per-coefficient threshold decoding with explicit modular normalisation
(Listing~\ref{lst:decode}).

\section{Bug 3: Weak Wrong-Key Resistance}

\textbf{File:} \texttt{frontend/lib/crypto/minikyber.ts}

\textbf{Root cause:} The shared secret seed was a single randomly chosen bit,
giving 1 bit of entropy. The shared secret was derived by hashing this 1-bit value
with the ciphertext.

\textbf{Consequence:} A wrong decapsulation (with a different secret key) would
recover a random value. With only two possible shared secrets (derived from $m=0$ or
$m=1$), the probability of a wrong-key decapsulation producing the same shared secret
as the correct decapsulation was 50\%. This completely undermines wrong-key resistance,
a fundamental KEM security property.

\textbf{Fix:} Encode a full random byte (8 bits) across 8 polynomial coefficients.
A wrong decapsulation must now match all 8 bits to produce the same shared secret.
Collision probability: $1/256 \approx 0.4\%$.

\section{Bug 4: Frodo Decode Failure}

\textbf{File:} \texttt{frontend/lib/crypto/minifrodo.ts}

\textbf{Root cause:} Identical to Bug 2: a single matrix element comparison against
$Q/4$ without modular normalisation, causing the same negative-noise wrap-around
misclassification.

\textbf{Fix:} The same $[Q/4, 3Q/4)$ per-element threshold applied to the Frodo
matrix element, with explicit normalisation to $[0, Q)$ before comparison.

\section{Significance of Bug Discovery}

The discovery of four bugs through automated testing---all invisible in integration
testing---has a broader methodological implication: cryptographic code requires
unit-level correctness verification that is qualitatively different from functional
integration testing. This is particularly acute for implementations involving modular
arithmetic, where semantically reasonable code (averaging coefficients, comparing to a
threshold) can be subtly wrong in ways that manifest only under specific noise
realisations. The 100-iteration correctness sweep is the minimum viable test for LWE
implementations: at a 55\% failure rate, the probability of all 100 tests passing
despite the bug is $(0.45)^{100} \approx 10^{-35}$---effectively zero.

\section{Design Principles}

The benchmarking methodology was designed to produce statistically valid, honest, and
reproducible results. The following design decisions were made explicitly:

\subsection{Consistent Execution Environment}

All four algorithms (Kyber, Frodo, NTRU, ECDH) are benchmarked within the same
JavaScript process, using the same \texttt{performance.now()} measurement
infrastructure, under the same JIT compilation conditions. This is the primary
advantage of pure TypeScript implementations over WASM-based alternatives: timing
differences between algorithms reflect only algorithmic differences, not environment
differences.

\subsection{JIT Warmup}

Pilot runs at 1,000 iterations showed 8--12\% higher mean operation times than runs
at 5,000 iterations, confirming that JIT optimisation is not complete at lower
iteration counts. A 50-iteration warmup phase precedes all measurements, allowing the
JIT to stabilise before any timing is recorded.

\subsection{Batched Timing and Effective Sample Size}

Individual operations are timed in batches of 10:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Java, caption={Batched timing in benchmark.ts}]
for (let batch = 0; batch < totalBatches; batch++) {
  const start = performance.now();
  for (let i = 0; i < BATCH_SIZE; i++) {
    await miniKyberKeyGen();
  }
  const elapsed = (performance.now() - start) / BATCH_SIZE;
  timings.push(elapsed); // one measurement per batch
}
\end{lstlisting}

This yields $n_{\text{eff}} = 500$ independent timing measurements from 5,000
invocations (500 batches of 10). Statistical analysis uses $n_{\text{eff}} = 500$.
Claiming $n = 5{,}000$ would incorrectly narrow confidence intervals by a factor of
$\sqrt{10} \approx 3.16$, overstating measurement precision.

\subsection{Session Throughput Benchmark}

The throughput benchmark simulates 500 independent sessions, each comprising a KEM
handshake followed by 100 AES-GCM encrypt-then-decrypt cycles on 500\,KB payloads.
This workload was designed to be representative of image and voice message sizes in
messaging applications. With $n = 500$ session observations per algorithm and observed
standard deviations of approximately 18--19\,ms, the study has statistical power
$> 0.99$ to detect differences of $\geq 1$\,ms at $\alpha = 0.05$.

\section{Statistical Analysis}

\subsection{Confidence Intervals}

All 95\% confidence intervals use the formula:
\[
\bar{x} \pm 1.96 \cdot \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n_{\text{eff}}}}
\]
where $\bar{x}$ is the sample mean, $\sigma$ the sample standard deviation, and
$n_{\text{eff}} = 500$.

\subsection{Welch's t-Test}

Pairwise comparisons use Welch's $t$-test~\cite{peikert2016}, which does not assume
equal variances:
\[
t = \frac{\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2}{\sqrt{s_1^2/n_1 + s_2^2/n_2}}
\]
with degrees of freedom given by the Welch-Satterthwaite equation:
\[
\nu = \frac{(s_1^2/n_1 + s_2^2/n_2)^2}
           {(s_1^2/n_1)^2/(n_1-1) + (s_2^2/n_2)^2/(n_2-1)}
\]

Welch's test is the correct choice because the algorithms exhibit substantially
different standard deviations (e.g., Kyber KeyGen $\sigma = 18.12\,\mu$s vs Frodo
KeyGen $\sigma = 32.02\,\mu$s). Student's $t$-test assumes equal variances; applying
it here would produce incorrect test statistics and potentially erroneous conclusions.

\subsection{Key Generation Analysis}

The key generation ranking (Kyber $>$ NTRU $>$ Frodo) reflects fundamental algorithmic
properties. Kyber's Ring-LWE structure enables efficient NTT-friendly polynomial
sampling. NTRU's key generation requires polynomial inversion---expensive at any
parameter size but more expensive relative to other operations at $N=7$ than at
$N=509$. Frodo's plain LWE requires matrix-vector operations over a full $n \times n$
matrix with no algebraic shortcuts.

The high standard deviations relative to means (e.g., Kyber KeyGen $\sigma/\mu \approx 5.3$)
reflect JIT variability: the JavaScript engine's optimisation level at any given
invocation varies, producing a bimodal distribution of fast (JIT-optimised) and
slower (interpreted) executions. The narrow confidence intervals despite high variance
demonstrate the benefit of $n_{\text{eff}} = 500$: the CI half-width for Kyber KeyGen
is $1.96 \times 18.12 / \sqrt{500} \approx 1.59\,\mu$s, confirming precise mean estimation.

\subsection{The Central Architectural Finding}

Table~\ref{tab:throughput} reveals the most important result: per-message encryption
time is essentially identical across all four algorithms ($\approx 5.5$\,ms encrypt,
$\approx 1.85$\,ms decrypt). This is because all algorithms use the same AES-256-GCM
cipher once the session key is established. The entire algorithmic difference manifests
only in the single key exchange event at session initiation---836\,$\mu$s for Kyber
vs 1.36\,ms for ECDH.

This has a direct implication for system design: PQC overhead is an amortised cost.
For a session exchanging 100 messages, the per-message PQC overhead is
$\leq 836\,\mu$s $/ 100 = 8.36\,\mu$s---utterly negligible. Applications should
maximise session reuse (persistent WebSocket connections, session resumption) rather
than treating PQC overhead as a per-message cost.

\subsection{Statistical vs Practical Significance}

The Kyber vs ECDH session comparison ($t = -7.44$, $p \ll 0.001$) illustrates the
critical distinction between statistical and practical significance. Statistical
significance indicates the difference is real and not a sampling artefact: with
$n = 500$ observations and $\sigma \approx 18$\,ms, even a 9\,ms true difference
consistently appears in the data. Practical significance asks whether the difference
matters for users: a 9\,ms improvement on a 730\,ms session represents 1.2\%---a
difference imperceptible to human perception.

The correct interpretation is not ``PQC is faster than ECDH'' (implying deployment
advantage) nor ``PQC is slower'' (implying performance cost), but rather: \emph{at
session level, post-quantum and classical key exchange are practically
indistinguishable}. This is the strongest possible result for PQC adoption: the
performance objection to migration is empirically eliminated.

The two non-significant results (Frodo vs NTRU KeyGen, $p \approx 0.11$; Kyber vs
Frodo Encap, $p \approx 0.66$) are reported honestly. Failure to reject the null
hypothesis does not imply equality; it indicates insufficient evidence to conclude
a difference exists. Both non-findings have substantive explanations: Frodo and NTRU
key generation involve different operations with similar complexity at Mini parameters;
Kyber and Frodo encapsulation both involve similar polynomial/matrix operations at the
Mini parameter scale.

\subsection{Explaining the Kyber Timing Gap}

Mini-Kyber encapsulation (134.20\,$\mu$s) is approximately 14$\times$ slower than
production Kyber-512 ($\sim 9.3\,\mu$s). Four factors explain this gap:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Naive vs NTT polynomial multiplication:} Production Kyber uses the
  Number Theoretic Transform with AVX2 SIMD instructions, achieving $O(n \log n)$
  polynomial multiplication with hardware parallelism. The Mini implementation uses a
  na\"{i}ve $O(n^2)$ loop (65,536 scalar multiplications at $n = 256$).

  \item \textbf{JIT vs ahead-of-time compilation:} Production C code is compiled with
  full optimisation (\texttt{-O3 -march=native}). JavaScript JIT optimises at runtime
  and cannot match static compilation quality.

  \item \textbf{Platform differences:} Production benchmarks typically run on x86-64
  with AVX2. These measurements ran on Apple M2 (ARM) in Safari.

  \item \textbf{Module rank (not parameter reduction):} The Mini-Kyber $n$ and $q$
  are identical to production. The only structural difference is module rank, which
  reduces the number of polynomial multiplications but not their cost per multiplication.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{The NTRU Encapsulation Discrepancy}
\label{sec:ntru-discrepancy}

The most theoretically interesting discrepancy is Mini-NTRU encapsulation (181.00\,$\mu$s)
being \emph{slower} than production NTRU ($\sim 95\,\mu$s) despite having vastly
smaller parameters ($N = 7$ vs $N = 509$). This is explained by the dominance of
polynomial inversion at small $N$.

At $N = 7$: the polynomial inversion $f^{-1} \pmod{q}$ using the extended Euclidean
algorithm dominates relative to the $O(N^2) = O(49)$ multiplication cost of $h \cdot r$.

At $N = 509$: inversion cost amortises---it is paid once during key generation. The
dominant operation in encapsulation is the multiplication $h \cdot r$, which scales
as $O(N^2) = O(259{,}081)$ for na\"{i}ve multiplication. NTRU's cyclic convolution is
highly optimised in production for this operation, making it fast despite the large $N$.

This parameter-scale effect is a genuine research finding: it demonstrates empirically
that which operation dominates an algorithm's performance profile depends on the
operating regime, and that performance characterisation at reduced parameters must
account for these shifts in operational dominance.

\section{Achievement of Research Objectives}

\textbf{RQ1 (Performance):} The throughput benchmark conclusively demonstrates that
PQC KEMs impose no measurable performance penalty at session level. All three
algorithms produce session throughputs within the 95\% confidence interval of ECDH.
Kyber is statistically significantly faster ($t = -7.44$, $p \ll 0.001$) with a 9\,ms
advantage that is practically negligible. RQ1 is answered affirmatively: the
performance objection to PQC migration in browser-based messaging is not supported
by the evidence.

\textbf{RQ2 (Rankings):} Key generation and key size rankings are fully preserved
relative to production literature. The encapsulation ranking discrepancy (NTRU) is
fully explained by parameter-scale effects on operational dominance. The methodology
of benchmarking at reduced parameters to infer relative rankings is validated for
two of three metrics, with the third providing a documented, theoretically justified
exception.

\section{Threats to Validity}

\subsection{Construct Validity}

The primary construct validity threat is the use of Mini implementations with reduced
parameters. This has two effects: (a) absolute timings are not comparable to production,
and (b) the relative rankings of encapsulation operations differ for NTRU due to
parameter-scale effects. Both are acknowledged and explained. The research objective
is explicitly comparative, not absolute, mitigating concern (a). Concern (b) is
addressed by the theoretical explanation of inversion cost dominance at small $N$.

\subsection{Internal Validity}

JIT variability introduces within-experiment noise. Mitigated by: 50-iteration warmup;
batched timing; $n_{\text{eff}} = 500$; and multiple runs confirming consistent mean
estimates despite high standard deviations.

\subsection{External Validity}

A single platform (Safari, Apple M2) was used. The key findings---that PQC overhead
concentrates in key exchange, not per-message encryption; and that algorithmic rankings
match production literature---are protocol and mathematical properties respectively,
not platform-dependent measurements. Both should hold across browser and hardware
combinations.

\subsection{In-Memory Simulation}

The throughput benchmark uses no network transmission. Network latency affects all
algorithms equally (all use identical AES-GCM for messages), so comparative findings
are unaffected. The benchmark deliberately isolates cryptographic cost.

\section{Security Analysis}

The Mini implementations provide no cryptographic security guarantees:
parameters are too small, JavaScript cannot guarantee constant-time execution,
and NTRU omits polynomial inversion mod $p$. However, they provide:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{CSPRNG randomness:} All random sampling uses \texttt{crypto.getRandomValues()}.
  The earlier \texttt{Math.random()} usage in Mini-NTRU was identified during security
  review and replaced.

  \item \textbf{Correct algorithmic structure:} The operations measured---polynomial
  multiplication, matrix-vector products, cyclic convolution---are the same operations
  that dominate production runtime.

  \item \textbf{Session security via AES-GCM:} All messages are encrypted with
  AES-256-GCM using HKDF-derived session keys. Message security does not depend on
  the Mini KEM's security properties.
\end{itemize}

The production wrapper (\texttt{production-kem.ts}) using \texttt{@noble/post-quantum}
provides full IND-CCA2 ML-KEM-768 with the Fujisaki-Okamoto transform for any
deployment context requiring genuine post-quantum security.

\chapter{Conclusion}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\section{Summary of Findings}

This dissertation has demonstrated the practical feasibility of post-quantum
cryptography in real-time browser-based messaging with three principal findings:

\textbf{1. PQC imposes no measurable performance penalty.}
Welch's $t$-tests on 500-session throughput benchmarks confirm that all three PQC
algorithms (Kyber, Frodo, NTRU) produce session times indistinguishable from ECDH at
the practical level. Kyber is statistically significantly faster ($t = -7.44$,
$p \ll 0.001$), but the 9\,ms absolute difference is imperceptible. The performance
objection to PQC migration is not supported by evidence.

\textbf{2. PQC overhead concentrates in session establishment, not messages.}
Per-message encryption is algorithm-agnostic (all algorithms produce $\approx 5.5$\,ms
encrypt and $\approx 1.85$\,ms decrypt times). The entire algorithmic difference
manifests in the single session establishment event. This architectural finding is
independent of parameter size and holds in production deployments.

\textbf{3. Automated correctness testing is essential for cryptographic implementations.}
Four bugs with failure rates up to 55\% were entirely invisible in integration testing.
The 100-iteration correctness sweep is the minimum viable validation for LWE
implementations, exposing modular arithmetic errors that surface only under specific
noise realisations.

\section{Future Work}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{WASM-based production benchmark:} Extending the benchmark to include
  liboqs-WASM implementations of FrodoKEM-640 and NTRU-hps2048509 would enable
  absolute timing comparisons with published literature, supplementing the relative
  comparisons provided here.

  \item \textbf{Known Answer Test (KAT) validation:} Verifying Mini implementations
  against NIST KAT vectors would provide formal correctness assurance beyond
  statistical testing.

  \item \textbf{Hybrid key exchange:} Implementing X25519+ML-KEM hybrid key exchange
  following the TLS~1.3 precedent~\cite{tls13} would provide the hedged security
  model recommended for early PQC deployments.

  \item \textbf{Cross-browser benchmarking:} The infrastructure in
  \texttt{cross-browser-benchmark.ts} supports Chrome and Firefox data collection.
  Extending the study to multiple browsers would strengthen external validity.

  \item \textbf{Formal security analysis:} A formal proof of the E2EE protocol under
  the symbolic model (e.g., using ProVerif or Tamarin) would provide security
  guarantees beyond the informal argument presented here.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Closing Remarks}

The quantum computing threat to public-key cryptography is real, its timeline is
uncertain, and the HNDL attack model makes migration urgent regardless of timeline.
This dissertation demonstrates that the technical barriers to PQC adoption in
browser-based messaging are lower than commonly assumed: the performance overhead is
negligible, the architectural changes are modest (a KEM replaces a Diffie-Hellman
exchange), and the engineering challenge is tractable with rigorous testing. The
quantum-resistant future does not require a performance compromise.
